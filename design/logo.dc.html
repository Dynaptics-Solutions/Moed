1Moed — five marks

Each one draws the same thing: a day with a visible limit. Every mark is flat geometry, works in one colour, and holds up at 16px — shown at the right of each card next to the app icon. Type is held constant so the mark is the only variable; alternative faces are at the end. These are drawn, not generated: I can refine geometry, weight and spacing on whichever you pick.

1aThe dial — the o is the day
M ed
16px
The name carries the idea. The o of Moed is the day, filled to two thirds. The mark is already inside the word, so the icon and the wordmark are the same object. It also animates: the ring fills as the day does.
1bThe load line — the mark that says no more
Moed
16px
Borrowed from ships. The Plimsoll line is painted on a hull to show the maximum safe load — the law that stopped ships being overfilled. Same argument as the app, and the fill stops short of the line, so the hull stays whole and the line reads as a limit.
1cThe span — an appointed time, measured
Moed
16px
A measurement, not a metaphor. Two end stops and a span between them, part used and part still free. Reads as a ruler or a bracket depending on the moment, and it sits under the wordmark as a rule rather than beside it.
1dThe gap — what is left of the day
Moed
16px
The simplest of the five. A whole day with a wedge still open. Survives any size, any medium, embroidery included — but it is one step from a generic pie chart, so it leans hardest on the wordmark to carry character.
1eThe bracket — a bounded day
Moed
16px
Typographic and restrained. Brackets are what you put around a fixed span. Nothing in this category looks like it — no checkmark, no calendar grid, no clock. The 16px version drops the interior split and keeps the shape.
The wordmark on its own

Four letters, so the face does most of the work. Held out separately because it is a decision on its own — and a Hebrew-derived name reads differently in a serif than in a grotesk.

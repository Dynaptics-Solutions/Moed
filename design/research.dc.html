Before the designs · August 2026

What is already out there, and what is missing

A scan of the daily-planner category on the App Store and Play Store, what each app is actually built around, where reviewers say they break down, and the feature set I would build from that. Sources are 2026 review round-ups and public user reviews, not hands-on testing — treat the scoring as a design read, not a benchmark.

01 — The field
Structured
Timeline
Tasks and calendar events on one vertical axis for the day, with week and month views, widgets and calendar sync. The clearest expression of "a day is a length of time" — the idea behind direction Rail.
Sunsama
Ritual
Drag tasks onto a time-blocked calendar, set a daily goal, and close the day with a reflection step. Almost alone in treating planning as a twice-daily ritual rather than a list you poke at.
Todoist
Task manager
The polish benchmark: natural-language entry, a "Today" view, recurring tasks, and Karma gamification. Reviewers note the calendar sits alongside as a separate view rather than a native unified one.
TickTick
Task manager+
The same job as Todoist with more surface area — habits, pomodoro, calendar. The standing summary is that Todoist is cleaner and TickTick is more feature-rich.
Any.do
Unified view
Sells itself directly on the problem: tasks in one app and calendar in another that never talk. Functional free tier, with calendar sync, recurring tasks and AI planning behind premium.
Notion
Workspace
Not a planner, a kit for building one: relational databases, timeline and calendar views, tens of thousands of templates. Reviewers consistently flag the flexibility as intimidating for beginners.
Google Calendar
Default
Free, universal, and the thing your app has to sync with rather than beat. Strong at events, deliberately weak at daily planning systems.
AI schedulers
Motion et al.
AI task prioritisation, AI calendar, AI meeting assistant. The loudest 2026 category and the one with the sharpest backlash — see gap 05.

03 — Where the category breaks down
01
The list and the calendar still live apart
Reviewers describe Todoist's calendar as a separate view rather than a native unified one, and Any.do markets its unified view specifically at people whose to-do app and calendar never talk. The problem is well known and still only half solved.
Our move — there is no task list and no calendar. There is one day.
02
Nothing shows you the day is full
Every app lets you add a tenth task to a day that holds six. Sunsama's daily goal and Journal it!'s time blocks gesture at capacity; none of them stop you, or show you what will not fit.
Our move — the day has a visible ceiling. Adding past it shows you what falls off the bottom.
03
Unfinished work vanishes or nags
Most planners either roll a missed task silently into tomorrow or turn it red forever. Both teach you to stop trusting the list. Sunsama's end-of-day reflection is the rare app that makes you look at what did not happen.
Our move — slippage is a first-class object with its own tray and a 20-second evening close-out.
04
Clean apps become cockpits
The pattern is documented across the category: launch focused, accumulate integrations and views, end up needing twenty minutes to learn. One 2026 review of Routine put it as overly complex yet not powerful — the worst of both.
Our move — a hard cap of four tabs and one capture affordance. Section 06 lists what we refuse to build.
05
AI is being added where it is not wanted
2026's headline features are AI prioritisation, AI calendars and AI meeting assistants. The counter-position is getting louder — one widely shared piece argues you do not need AI to tell you what to do today, because you already know.
Our move — no auto-scheduling. AI only parses what you type, and only on capture.
06
The paywall lands after you have moved in
A recurring review complaint across planning apps: basic planning, sync or export turns out to be paid only after you have invested the setup time, with no clear statement up front of what stays free.
Our move — planning is free forever and works offline. Paid is sync across devices and shared projects, stated on the sign-up screen.
04 — The features, in plain words

One sentence each, no jargon. Grouped by whether it ships at launch or after.

Group A · Ships at launch
Nobody will switch without these
01
Your calendar shows up here
Connect Google or Apple Calendar and your existing events appear in the day. Anything you add in the app goes back to them.
02
Type it the way you would say it
Write "dentist thu 2pm" in one box and it files itself on Thursday at two. No form to fill in.
03
Things that repeat, repeat
Set a task once for every Monday, the first of the month, or every third day.
04
Reminders before, not on the day
A notification fifteen minutes before the thing, not a red badge the morning of.
05
Three ways to look at it
Today, the week, or by project. Same information, different zoom.
06
Today on your home screen
A widget with the next few items, so most days you never open the app.
07
Works with no signal
Everything lives on the phone. Signing in only copies it to your other devices.
Group B · Ships at launch
The four that make it worth switching
1 · The day has a ceiling
The app counts hours left in your day, not tasks left on a list. Add a seventh task to a day that holds six and it shows you which one falls off the bottom — before you commit to it.
Fixes the thing every planner lets you do: promise yourself fourteen hours of work.
2 · Nothing disappears quietly
A task you did not get to moves into a tray you can see, with a count on it. It never slides silently into tomorrow, and it never turns red and stays red.
You keep trusting the list, which is the whole point of having one.
3 · Two minutes at the end of the day
Each evening the app shows what is left with three buttons per item — done, move, drop. Work through it and the day is closed.
Tomorrow starts from a decision you made, not from yesterday's leftovers.
4 · Move one thing, not five
Drag a task to Thursday and its length, its reminder and its place in the day go with it. There is no cleanup afterwards.
Rescheduling is the most common action in a planner and the one most apps make painful.

07 — What this means for the three directions
Rail
Strongest fit. Capacity and slippage are already the visual language — the rail shows hours, the unscheduled tray is the slippage tray. Closest competitor is Structured, so the bar for polish is high.
Ledger
Reads as calm and trustworthy, which is the honest answer to gap 04. Needs a way to show capacity that is not a timeline — a filled-hours bar under the date, for instance.
Deck
Best at one-thing-at-a-time focus and at the close-out ritual, which is naturally a card you swipe. Weakest at showing a whole week's capacity at a glance.
08 — Your template idea — and how I would change it

The instinct is right: not everything in a planner is the same shape. A morning routine is genuinely not a task. But Category + Type as two things the user assembles is one concept too many — that is the Notion trap from section 01, where flexibility becomes homework.

As proposed
User creates a Category, then picks a Type from a list.
Two decisions before anything exists, both made when the user knows least. And a checklist has no duration, so it cannot sit on the rail — the two ideas collide.
As I would build it
Types are the engine. Templates are the interface.
The user picks "Morning routine" off a short shelf and edits it. The type is already set correctly underneath. Nobody chooses a type from a menu on day one.
The four types, and why each earns its place

A type only exists if it behaves differently on the rail. Anything that is only a different colour or icon is a tag, not a type.

Task
v1
One thing, one length, one block on the rail.
The default. Everything is this unless it needs to be something else.
Routine
v1
A checklist that repeats and resets — and occupies one block, not ten.
This is your "Morning Routine". Six ticks inside a single 40-minute block, so it costs the day 40 minutes, not six entries. Resets each morning.
Session
v1
Timed work with no finish line. Done means time spent.
Reading, practice, admin. You cannot tick it off, you can only give it an hour. Pairs with the focus timer from section 05.
Errand
v2
No time of its own, tied to a place, batches with others nearby.
Groceries, post office, pharmacy. The app groups them into one trip rather than three orphan tasks with no duration.
Appointment
auto
Fixed, cannot be moved by you, comes from the calendar.
Not user-creatable — it appears because your calendar says so. Listed here because it is the fifth behaviour on the rail.
The template shelf
Eight to start with. Not a gallery.
Notion ships tens of thousands of templates and reviewers call it intimidating. A short shelf that covers the common cases is worth more than a searchable library.
Morning routine · routine Shutdown · routine Deep work · session Weekly review · routine Errand run · errand Workout · routine Ship a feature · task set Travel day · mixed
"Save this day as a template" is the ninth entry, and it is how the shelf grows without us curating one.
09 — AI — where it earns its place, and where it does not

Section 03 gap 05 is the position: no auto-scheduling. That is not anti-AI, it is a rule about who decides. One line, applied to every feature below.

AI may propose. It may never move anything on its own, and every proposal must be undoable in one tap.
Six uses that pass that test
Parse what you type
v1
"dentist thu 2pm, 25 min drive" becomes a dated block with travel time attached.
Already in v1. Pure typing-saved; the result is visible before you confirm it.
Say it instead
v1
Hold the capture button, talk, get three structured items you can edit.
The highest-value AI on a phone, because the alternative is typing while walking.
How long this usually takes you
v1
Suggests a duration from your own history of similar work, and says why.
"You have run this 4 times, averaging 40m." Fixes the hardest part of a capacity model — people cannot estimate.
Break it down
v2
On demand, turns "Draft Q4 roadmap" into five editable steps as a routine or task set.
Only when asked, never on capture. Otherwise every one-line task becomes a project.
Explain the slippage
v2
At the weekly review: "this moved six times, always on days with a meeting before 10."
An observation about your own data, not advice. This is the one that makes the tray useful instead of guilt-inducing.
Move things by sentence
v2
"Push everything after lunch by an hour." Shows the result, waits for a tap.
Typed rescheduling from section 05. It only does what you asked.
And what it will not do
Fill your day for you Rank your priorities Rewrite your task titles A chat panel Attend your meetings
Every one of these exists in a competitor. Every one of them is the reason the 2026 backlash is happening.
10 — Three more features worth adding
Someday, separate from slipped
v1
Things with no date and no intention yet live somewhere other than the slippage tray.
Right now the tray would fill with wishes and stop meaning anything. Slipped means you meant to and did not. Someday means you have not decided.
Capacity check on invites
v2
Accepting a meeting shows what it costs that day before you accept.
The main way days get overfull is other people. A capacity model that ignores invites is only half a model.
One trip, several errands
v2
Errands near each other collapse into a single block with a route.
Follows directly from the Errand type. Turns four orphan tasks into one 40-minute thing you will actually do.
11 — What each type actually changes

This is the part that justifies having types at all: a different sheet when you create it, and different behaviour when it reaches the day. Everything shares a title, a project and notes — only the rows below differ.

Two rules that hold across all of them
1 · A duration is required on everything you create. The ceiling cannot work without it, so it is the one field we will not let you skip.
2 · Only things with a time write out to your real calendar. Anything in the tray stays inside the app, so we never fill a shared calendar with your to-do list.

12 — Meals, events, expenses: should we?
Say the awkward part first
Adding three new domains is precisely the pattern gap 04 describes: a focused app launches, accumulates areas, becomes a cockpit. If we do this the way most apps do it — three more tabs — we will have built the thing we said we would not build.
There is one version of this that works, and it depends on a single idea holding.
The idea that makes it one app
Everything you plan spends one of two things: hours, or money.
We already built a ceiling for hours. Money is the same mechanic with a different unit — a weekly cap, a bar that fills, and a screen that tells you what will not fit. Meals and events are not new domains; they are things that draw down one or both budgets.
If a feature cannot be expressed as spending hours or money, it does not belong in this app.
Meal planning
Yes — as a type
Dinner is already a Routine: a repeating block of a known length. Deciding it in advance is planning; cooking it costs 40 minutes; the ingredients are an Errand run. All three mechanics exist.
Build it as a Meal type — a routine with a shopping list attached. Planning the week generates one errand run and five 40-minute blocks. That is the whole feature.
Risk · Do not build a recipe database, nutrition tracking or macro counting. That is Mealime's product, it needs content operations we do not have, and reviewers of meal apps complain most about clunky imports and locked exports.
Expense tracking
Yes — as a second ceiling
This is the strongest fit of the three, because it is the same screen we already designed. A weekly spend cap, a bar, and a sheet that says "this puts you 40 over" is S2 with pesos instead of minutes.
Build the ceiling, not the ledger. You set a weekly cap, log what you spend in one line, and the app tells you what is left. Meals and events draw from it automatically.
Risk · Do not build bank sync, receipt scanning, or categories-of-categories. That is YNAB, it needs regulated integrations, and it doubles the surface area of the app on its own.
Event planning
No — you already have it
An event is a project with a fixed date, a countdown, a task list and a budget. We have projects, dates, tasks and — with the above — a budget. There is nothing left to build.
Add a due date and a spend cap to Projects, and a countdown on the project screen. A birthday, a launch and a trip are all the same object.
Risk · A real event planner — guest lists, RSVPs, vendors, seating — is a different product for different people. Building it would be the moment this stops being a planner.
What the app becomes

Two ceilings, four types, one shelf of templates. Not five apps in a drawer.

Ceiling one
Hours in a day
Tasks, routines, sessions, errands, meals. Everything that takes time.
Ceiling two
Money in a week
Groceries, events, anything with a price. Everything that costs.
My recommendation on sequencing
Ship v1 as hours only, exactly as designed. Add the money ceiling as v1.1 — it reuses the screens, so it is cheap, and it is the feature that makes the app feel bigger than a to-do list. Meals follow it, because meals need the shopping list to have somewhere to charge. Events need nothing beyond a date and a cap on Projects.
Doing all four at launch is how the thing becomes a cockpit before anyone has used it once.
One knock-on
If money is in scope, this is no longer a "daily planner" and the naming shortlist changes with it. Day-prefixed names — Daycap, Dayspan, Daykeep — get narrower than the product. Something about capacity or room would carry both ceilings.
13 — Bills, and everything else you could plan

Bills are the best idea in this conversation so far, because they are not a new feature. They are the money version of something we already designed.

The symmetry
An appointment is a fixed cost in hours. A bill is a fixed cost in money.
Neither can be moved by you, both have a date, and both lower the ceiling before you have decided anything. Your real money for the week is not the cap — it is the cap minus what is already promised. That is exactly how the day works once appointments are on the rail.
Fields on the sheet
Amount required
Due date required
Repeats — monthly, quarterly
Fixed or variable amount
Alert me — days before
Autopay on or off
How it behaves
It appears on the day it is due and reduces that week's money ceiling from the moment you add it. A variable bill reserves last month's amount until you confirm the real one.
Alerts follow the same rule as the day: "leave by" becomes "pay by". One notification, timed so you can still act, not a badge that lives forever.
An unpaid bill goes to the slippage tray with the number of days it has slipped — the same object, counting money instead of minutes.
Autopay bills still show, but greyed and un-actionable — the point of listing them is that they have already taken the money, not that you need to do something.
Everything else you could plan, run through the test

One question decides each: can it be expressed as spending hours or money? If yes, it needs no new machinery and it is nearly free to add. If no, it is a different product.

Passes · costs us almost nothing
Subscriptions
Bills you forgot you had. A bill with a flag, and a yearly total on the review screen. This one sells itself.
Savings goals
The money ceiling inverted — a floor you protect rather than a cap you spend to. Same bar, other direction.
Debt payoff
A repeating bill with an end date and a countdown. Nothing new.
Trips
A project with a date, a spend cap and a packing routine. All three exist.
Home upkeep
Routines with long intervals — filters, servicing, renewals. The repeat engine already handles it.
Study and exams
Sessions with a deadline. Already covered by the Session type.
Medication
A routine with hard times and no flexibility. Add it, but design the reminders carefully — a missed dose is not a missed task.
Fails · a different product
Recipes and nutrition
Needs a content operation and a database we would have to maintain forever. Meals work without it.
Guest lists and RSVPs
Event logistics is its own category with its own users. Nothing to do with capacity.
Bank and card sync
Regulated integrations, ongoing compliance, and it turns a planner into a finance app.
Investments
Live market data, tax implications, and a duty of care we do not want.
Team projects
Assignment, permissions, comments. This is the Microsoft Planner problem from section 01.
Journaling and mood
Nothing is spent. Legitimate product, wrong app.
Where this leaves the roadmap
v1 hours only — the six screens already designed. v1.1 the money ceiling, and bills with it, because bills are the reason anyone would set a cap in the first place. v1.2 meals and subscriptions, which are both a type on top of what v1.1 built. Trips, savings and upkeep after that, in whatever order users ask for them.
Nine of the ten things in the left column need no new concepts. That is the test working — and it is the only reason this can grow without becoming the cockpit.
